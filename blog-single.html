Why we let kids break the robot

By Sana Iqbal · 28 August 2026 · 6 min read · For parents

🧠

There is a moment in every class that used to make me uncomfortable. A child finishes wiring their robot, presses the button, and nothing happens. Their shoulders drop. They look up at the mentor, waiting to be rescued.

For the first year of running this club, we rescued them. We would lean in, spot the loose jumper wire in about four seconds, push it back into the breadboard, and watch the robot spring to life. The child would smile. We would feel useful. Everyone went home happy.

It took us far too long to notice that those children were not getting better at anything.

What we do instead

Now the rule is simple: the mentor never touches the hardware. We ask questions instead of pointing. The first one is always the same, and it is almost never about the robot.

  • What did you expect it to do?
  • What did it actually do?
  • What is the smallest thing you could test to find the difference?

That third question is the whole method. It turns a vague feeling of “it’s broken” into a testable guess. A nine-year-old who can form a testable guess is doing real engineering, whatever the robot ends up doing.

The child who fixes their own loose wire remembers it for a year. The child whose wire I fixed forgets by the following Tuesday.
— something I should have worked out much sooner

The ten uncomfortable minutes

Between the robot failing and the child finding the fault, there is a stretch of maybe ten minutes that is genuinely uncomfortable to watch. They try things that will not work. They get frustrated. Some of them want to give up.

Those ten minutes are the class. Everything before them was assembly, and everything after them is celebration. The learning happens in the middle, and it only happens if we can sit still through it.

🔍

A jammed servo, forty minutes before judging. They found it.

What this looks like at home

Parents ask how to do this outside the lab, usually while their child is halfway through a Lego set or a science kit. The honest answer is that it is harder at home, because you are tired and it would be faster to just do it.

Try this instead of helping

Ask “what have you already tried?” and then stay quiet for longer than feels natural. Most children answer the question themselves in the silence.

When we do step in

This is not a rule about letting children struggle indefinitely. We step in when three things are true: the child has genuinely run out of ideas, the frustration has stopped being productive, and there is something dangerous or expensive about to happen.

Even then, we hand back the smallest possible clue. Not “this wire is loose” but “check the power side of the board first.” They still get to find it.

The part nobody expects

The change we did not predict is what happens the following week. Children who have debugged something themselves start volunteering to help other children debug. They ask the same three questions we asked them, in the same order, often word for word. By the end of a term, half the debugging in the room is being done by nine-year-olds.

That is the whole point. We are not trying to produce children who can build one specific robot. We are trying to produce children who are not afraid of the moment something stops working — because that moment is most of engineering, and most of everything else too.
